This report presents the experimental results and analysis for the CENG 467 Take-Home Midterm. Five NLP tasks are addressed: text classification, named entity recognition, text summarization, machine translation, and language modeling. For each task, multiple modeling approaches are implemented and compared under consistent experimental conditions.

\section{Question 1: Representation Learning in Text Classification}

\subsection{Dataset}

The IMDb sentiment analysis dataset~\citep{maas2011imdb} consists of 50{,}000 movie reviews evenly split into 25{,}000 training and 25{,}000 test samples, with balanced positive and negative labels. From the training set, 10\% (2{,}500 samples) is held out as a validation set, leaving 22{,}500 samples for training.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Tokenization}

Two tokenization strategies are compared:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Whitespace tokenization with normalization}: HTML tags are stripped, text is lowercased, punctuation is removed, and tokens are split on whitespace. A vocabulary of 30{,}000 most frequent tokens is built from the training set. Out-of-vocabulary tokens are mapped to \texttt{<UNK>}. This strategy is used for the TF-IDF and BiLSTM models.

    \item \textbf{WordPiece subword tokenization}: The DistilBERT tokenizer splits rare words into subword units, handling morphological variations and out-of-vocabulary words without an explicit UNK mapping. Maximum sequence length is set to 256 tokens, matching the truncation applied to other models for fair comparison.
\end{enumerate}

Key differences are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:tokenization}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Tokenization strategy comparison.}
\label{tab:tokenization}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Vocab Size} & \textbf{OOV Handling} & \textbf{Used By} \\
\midrule
Whitespace + normalization & 30{,}000 & \texttt{<UNK>} token & TF-IDF, BiLSTM \\
WordPiece (subword) & 30{,}522 & Subword split & DistilBERT \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Preprocessing decisions such as stopword removal were deliberately omitted for the TF-IDF model, as negation words (e.g., \textit{not}, \textit{never}) carry strong sentiment signal. Truncation to 256 tokens affects BiLSTM more than TF-IDF, since TF-IDF operates on the full bag-of-words representation.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{TF-IDF + Logistic Regression}: A \texttt{TfidfVectorizer} with unigrams and bigrams (\texttt{ngram\_range=(1,2)}), 50{,}000 maximum features, and sublinear TF scaling is paired with Logistic Regression (\texttt{C=1.0}, \texttt{max\_iter=1000}).

\textbf{BiLSTM + GloVe}: A bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 128 is initialized with 100-dimensional GloVe embeddings~\citep{pennington2014glove} (29{,}397 of 30{,}000 vocabulary tokens found). Dropout of 0.3 is applied before the classification head. Training uses Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 32, and early stopping with patience 2.

\textbf{DistilBERT}: \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased}~\citep{sanh2019distilbert} is fine-tuned for sequence classification using the HuggingFace \texttt{Trainer} API. Training uses AdamW with learning rate $2 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.01, batch size 32, and 3 epochs with early stopping.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{table}[H]

\label{tab:q1_results}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

\midrule
TF-IDF + Logistic Regression & 0.9064 & 0.8985 & 0.8985 \\
BiLSTM + GloVe               & 0.8852 & 0.8528 & 0.8526 \\
DistilBERT (fine-tuned)      & 0.9052 & \textbf{0.9115} & \textbf{0.9115} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

DistilBERT achieves the highest test accuracy (91.15\%) and macro-F1 (0.9115), followed by TF-IDF (89.85\%) and BiLSTM (85.28\%). Notably, TF-IDF outperforms BiLSTM despite being a much simpler model. This is attributable to two factors: (1) TF-IDF with bigrams captures local phrase-level sentiment cues across the full document without truncation, while BiLSTM is limited to 256 tokens; (2) the BiLSTM hidden state bottleneck loses long-range context in lengthy reviews.

\subsection{Misclassification Analysis}

All three models share a common failure mode: reviews with \textit{mixed sentiment}, where the reviewer expresses genuine appreciation for specific elements (acting, atmosphere, nostalgia) while rating the film negatively overall.

Representative error patterns:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Praise-then-criticize structure}: Reviews that open with positive statements before turning negative (e.g., praising Van Damme films generally before criticizing the specific movie) mislead bag-of-words models.
    \item \textbf{Irony and hedging}: Phrases like ``guilty pleasure'', ``not too bad'', and ``compared to garbage nowadays this isn't bad'' contain positive surface tokens despite negative intent.
    \item \textbf{Domain-specific sentiment}: References to beloved cult actors (Hasselhoff, Ida Lupino) trigger positive associations even in negative reviews that use them as partial compensation.
    \item \textbf{Conditional positivity}: ``The only reason this is not a 1 is the acting'' — a strongly negative review with embedded praise confuses all three models.
    \item \textbf{Nostalgia framing}: Reviews about childhood favorites use warm language regardless of objective quality assessment.
\end{enumerate}

DistilBERT makes the fewest errors (2{,}213 vs.\ 2{,}537 for TF-IDF and 3{,}680 for BiLSTM), demonstrating that contextual embeddings better capture negation scope and discourse structure.

\subsection{Discussion}

Sparse TF-IDF representations excel at capturing discriminative n-gram features and scale well to long documents, but lack positional and contextual awareness. Dense GloVe-initialized BiLSTM representations capture sequential dependencies but suffer from the fixed-length hidden state bottleneck and truncation sensitivity. Contextual DistilBERT representations, conditioned on the full token sequence via self-attention, best handle sentiment ambiguity and long-range negation.

In terms of interpretability, TF-IDF coefficients directly indicate which n-grams drive predictions, making it the most transparent model. BiLSTM attention weights provide partial interpretability. DistilBERT is a black box without additional attribution methods.

\section{Question 2: Named Entity Recognition}

\subsection{Dataset}

The CoNLL-2003 English NER dataset~\citep{tjong2003conll} contains newswire articles annotated with four entity types: Person (PER), Organization (ORG), Location (LOC), and Miscellaneous (MISC). The dataset provides official train, validation, and test splits containing 14{,}041, 3{,}250, and 3{,}453 sentences respectively. Entities are annotated in BIO (Beginning-Inside-Outside) format, resulting in 9 distinct tags: \texttt{O}, \texttt{B-PER}, \texttt{I-PER}, \texttt{B-ORG}, \texttt{I-ORG}, \texttt{B-LOC}, \texttt{I-LOC}, \texttt{B-MISC}, \texttt{I-MISC}.

\subsection{Preprocessing and BIO Tagging}

Tokens are lowercased for the BiLSTM-CRF model, and a word vocabulary of 30{,}000 tokens is built from the training set. GloVe 100-dimensional embeddings cover 18{,}415 of 21{,}011 vocabulary tokens. Sequences are padded or truncated to a maximum length of 128 tokens, which covers virtually all CoNLL sentences.

For BERT, the \texttt{bert-base-cased} tokenizer is used without lowercasing, since capitalization is a strong NER signal (e.g., ``Apple'' vs.\ ``apple''). Subword tokenization introduces a token alignment challenge: when a word is split into multiple subword tokens, the BIO label is assigned only to the first subword, and the remaining subwords receive a special \texttt{-100} label that is ignored during loss computation. This ensures entity labels remain word-aligned despite subword splitting.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{BiLSTM-CRF}: A bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 256 is initialized with GloVe 100-dimensional embeddings (trainable). A linear layer maps the BiLSTM output to emission scores, which are decoded by a CRF layer that enforces valid BIO tag transitions. Training uses Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, gradient clipping at 5.0, batch size 32, and early stopping with patience 3 on validation F1.

\textbf{BERT for Token Classification}: \texttt{bert-base-cased}~\citep{devlin2019bert} is fine-tuned with a token classification head using the HuggingFace \texttt{Trainer} API. Training uses AdamW with learning rate $2 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.01, batch size 16, and 3 epochs.

BERT outperforms BiLSTM-CRF by 9.6 F1 points overall (91.2\% vs.\ 81.6\%). The gap is largest for PER (+15 points) and ORG (+10 points), where capitalization and contextual disambiguation are most important. MISC remains the hardest category for both models (BiLSTM-CRF: 0.75, BERT: 0.79) due to its heterogeneous nature, encompassing nationalities, event names, and sports terms.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Both models share common failure modes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Entity type confusion}: ``CHINA'' appears as both LOC and PER depending on context (e.g., soccer team vs.\ country). BiLSTM-CRF predicts LOC in all cases, while BERT occasionally predicts ORG for team references.
    \item \textbf{Boundary errors}: Multi-token entities such as ``1995 World Cup'' lose their B- prefix token, causing partial span detection. BERT handles these better due to self-attention over the full sentence.
    \item \textbf{Single-token entities missed}: Short names like ``Bitar'' (a person) are consistently labeled O by BiLSTM-CRF when appearing without surrounding context, while BERT sometimes confuses them with ORG.
    \item \textbf{MISC ambiguity}: ``Soviet'', ``Asian'', ``Uzbek'' are categorized as MISC in CoNLL but share surface features with LOC and ORG, causing frequent misclassification in both models.
    \item \textbf{I- without B-}: BiLSTM-CRF occasionally predicts \texttt{I-MISC} without a preceding \texttt{B-MISC} in headline-style tokens, which the CRF layer should prevent but occasionally fails on rare tag sequences.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discussion}

The CRF layer in BiLSTM-CRF enforces global tag sequence constraints (e.g., \texttt{I-PER} cannot follow \texttt{B-ORG}), which improves over a plain softmax classifier. However, GloVe embeddings are context-independent: ``Washington'' has the same representation whether it refers to a person or a city. BERT's self-attention mechanism captures this ambiguity by conditioning each token's representation on its full sentence context, leading to substantially better entity disambiguation and boundary detection.

\section{Question 3: Text Summarization}

\subsection{Dataset}

The CNN/DailyMail dataset~\citep{hermann2015cnndm} consists of news articles paired with human-written bullet-point summaries. The test split is used for evaluation; 1{,}000 samples are randomly selected (seed 42) to keep inference time manageable. Average article length is 676 words; average reference summary length is 55 words.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{TextRank (Extractive)}: A graph-based extractive method~\citep{mihalcea2004textrank}. Sentences are represented as TF-IDF vectors; cosine similarity between all sentence pairs forms a weighted graph. PageRank (damping factor 0.85) ranks sentences by centrality, and the top 3 are returned in original order. No model parameters are involved.

\textbf{BART (Abstractive)}: \texttt{facebook/bart-large-cnn}~\citep{lewis2020bart} is a sequence-to-sequence transformer pre-trained on CNN/DailyMail with a denoising objective. Inference uses beam search with 4 beams, minimum output length 30, and maximum output length 128 tokens. The model is used off-the-shelf without fine-tuning.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Summarization metrics on CNN/DailyMail test set (1{,}000 samples).}
\label{tab:q3_results}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{ROUGE-1} & \textbf{ROUGE-2} & \textbf{ROUGE-L} & \textbf{BLEU} & \textbf{METEOR} & \textbf{BERTScore} \\
\midrule
TextRank (Extractive) & 0.3550 & 0.1415 & 0.2317 & 0.0921 & 0.3338 & 0.8637 \\
BART (Abstractive)    & \textbf{0.4408} & \textbf{0.2127} & \textbf{0.3103} & \textbf{0.1700} & \textbf{0.3911} & \textbf{0.8803} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

BART outperforms TextRank on all six metrics. The largest gap is on ROUGE-2 (+7.1 points), which measures bigram overlap and reflects BART's ability to produce fluent, locally coherent phrases that match reference phrasing. BLEU is low for both models, which is expected for summarization since multiple valid paraphrases exist and BLEU penalizes deviation from the reference surface form.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Three representative examples reveal characteristic behavior differences:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Medical marijuana article}: TextRank selects three scattered sentences (``I see this medical marijuana revolution in surprising places'', ``Your medical marijuana questions answered'', ``They want marijuana to be rescheduled'') that lack coherent flow. BART produces a coherent paragraph attributing claims to a named author and citing a specific statistic (53\% favor legalization), demonstrating its ability to synthesize information across paragraphs.

    \item \textbf{Child gang Twitter article}: TextRank produces long verbatim sentences with good lexical overlap but redundant content. BART generates a shorter, more informative summary that includes key facts (3{,}000 followers, Memphis origin) while paraphrasing rather than copying.

    \item \textbf{Chris Christie town hall article}: TextRank captures a fragment of dialogue (``You do know that?'') that is uninformative without context. BART correctly identifies the central event (teacher questioning Christie about ExxonMobil settlement) and names the participants, demonstrating stronger discourse understanding.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Error Analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TextRank sentence fragmentation}: Short articles with few sentences cause TextRank to return nearly the full text, producing over-long summaries with low density.
    \item \textbf{TextRank topic drift}: The TF-IDF similarity graph weights frequent terms heavily; in articles with repetitive boilerplate (sports scores, stock updates), TextRank selects template sentences rather than the main event.
    \item \textbf{BART hallucination}: BART occasionally introduces specific names or statistics not present in the source (e.g., attributing a quote to ``John Sutter'' in the marijuana article, while the actual author is Sanjay Gupta). This is a known failure mode of abstractive models.
    \item \textbf{BART entity confusion}: In multi-entity articles (e.g., political pieces with several named officials), BART sometimes conflates speakers or misattributes statements.
    \item \textbf{BLEU insensitivity}: Both models score poorly on BLEU despite producing readable summaries, confirming that BLEU is poorly calibrated for open-ended generation tasks.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discussion}

Extractive methods like TextRank guarantee factual faithfulness since every token comes from the source, but produce summaries that may lack coherence and miss the article's main point when key information is distributed across multiple passages. Abstractive models like BART generate fluent, concise summaries by paraphrasing and fusing information, at the cost of occasional hallucination. The ROUGE and BERTScore gap (ROUGE-1: +8.6 points, BERTScore: +1.7 points) confirms that abstractive generation better approximates human reference summaries on this dataset.

\section{Question 4: Machine Translation}

\subsection{Dataset}

The Multi30k dataset~\citep{elliott2016multi30k} contains 29{,}000 training, 1{,}014 validation, and 1{,}000 test sentence pairs in English and German, drawn from image captions. Sentences are short (average 13 tokens) and describe visual scenes.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{Seq2Seq + Bahdanau Attention}: A custom encoder-decoder architecture trained from scratch. The encoder is a bidirectional GRU (hidden size 512, embedding dim 256); the decoder is a unidirectional GRU augmented with additive (Bahdanau) attention~\citep{bahdanau2015attention} over all encoder hidden states. Vocabularies are built from the training set (min frequency 2): source 7{,}704 tokens, target 9{,}597 tokens. Training uses Adam ($lr=10^{-3}$), teacher forcing ratio 0.5, gradient clipping at 1.0, batch size 64, and early stopping with patience 3 on validation loss.

\textbf{MarianMT (Abstractive)}: \texttt{Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-de}~\citep{tiedemann2020opus} is a pretrained Marian NMT model fine-tuned on millions of parallel sentences. Inference-only, batch size 32, max length 128.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Translation metrics on Multi30k test set (1{,}000 sentence pairs, EN$\rightarrow$DE).}
\label{tab:q4_results}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{BLEU} & \textbf{METEOR} & \textbf{ChrF} & \textbf{BERTScore} \\
\midrule
Seq2Seq + Bahdanau Attention & 0.0154 & 0.3827 & 0.3453 & 0.6687 \\
MarianMT (opus-mt-en-de)     & \textbf{0.3625} & \textbf{0.6668} & \textbf{0.6428} & \textbf{0.8967} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

MarianMT dominates on all metrics. The BLEU gap is dramatic (0.36 vs.\ 0.015), reflecting that the Seq2Seq model trained from scratch on only 29{,}000 sentence pairs cannot learn reliable translation. MarianMT's METEOR (0.667) and BERTScore (0.897) confirm high-quality, semantically faithful translations.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Three test examples reveal the behavioral gap:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simple caption}: For ``A man in an orange hat starring at something,'' MarianMT produces a near-perfect translation. Seq2Seq generates a repetitive, incoherent output (``ein orangefarbenen hut auf einem orangefarbenen hut''), repeating n-grams without terminating properly.

    \item \textbf{Compound noun}: ``Boston Terrier running on lush green grass in front of a white fence'' — MarianMT correctly translates ``lush green grass'' as ``üppig grünem Gras.'' Seq2Seq produces a fragmented repetition of ``grünes gras'' and introduces the unrelated word ``auto,'' indicating vocabulary confusion.

    \item \textbf{Domain-specific term}: ``Karate uniform breaking a stick with a front kick'' — MarianMT handles the compound ``front kick'' as ``Front-Kick'' (a reasonable borrowing). Seq2Seq produces \texttt{<unk>} tokens for rare words like ``karate'' and ``kick,'' showing OOV sensitivity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Error Analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Seq2Seq repetition}: The model frequently enters repetition loops, generating the same phrase multiple times before hitting \texttt{max\_len}. This is a known GRU decoder failure mode when attention weights collapse to a single position.
    \item \textbf{Seq2Seq OOV}: Rare words (``karate'', ``terrier'', ``kick'') map to \texttt{<unk>}, degrading BLEU severely. Character-level or BPE tokenization would mitigate this.
    \item \textbf{Seq2Seq low BLEU vs.\ reasonable METEOR}: METEOR (0.383) is substantially higher than BLEU (0.015) for Seq2Seq. METEOR uses stemming and synonym matching, so partially correct words still score; BLEU requires exact n-gram matches and penalizes length mismatch.
    \item \textbf{MarianMT false friends}: MarianMT occasionally uses English loanwords in German output (``Front-Kick'') instead of native German equivalents. This is acceptable but reflects training corpus bias toward technical and informal text.
    \item \textbf{Data scale mismatch}: Seq2Seq is trained on 29{,}000 pairs; MarianMT was pre-trained on tens of millions. The comparison is intentionally asymmetric — its purpose is to demonstrate the effect of pre-training scale on translation quality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discussion}

The results confirm the central lesson of neural machine translation: data scale and pre-training are decisive. A Seq2Seq model with Bahdanau attention is architecturally sound (it introduced the attention mechanism that inspired Transformers), but requires far more data than Multi30k to produce fluent translations. MarianMT's BLEU of 0.36 on Multi30k matches published baselines for strong supervised models on this benchmark. The Seq2Seq model does learn some structure — its METEOR of 0.38 shows partial lexical correctness — but cannot generalize to the full vocabulary or produce grammatically coherent output without much more training data.

\section{Question 5: Language Modeling}

\subsection{Dataset}

WikiText-2~\citep{merity2017wikitext} is a word-level language modeling benchmark derived from verified Wikipedia articles. It contains 2{,}051{,}910 training tokens, 213{,}886 validation tokens, and 241{,}211 test tokens. The vocabulary built from the training set with minimum frequency 2 yields 39{,}275 unique tokens.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{N-gram LM (Trigram)}: A count-based trigram language model with Laplace (add-1) smoothing. Given context $(w_{i-2}, w_{i-1})$, the probability of $w_i$ is:
\[
P(w_i \mid w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) = \frac{C(w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}, w_i) + 1}{C(w_{i-2}, w_{i-1}) + |V|}
\]
where $|V|$ is the vocabulary size. No neural parameters are involved.

\textbf{LSTM LM}: A 2-layer LSTM language model with embedding dimension 256, hidden dimension 512, and dropout 0.5. At each time step the model predicts the next token given all previous tokens in the sequence. Training uses Adam ($lr=10^{-3}$), sequence length 128, batch size 32, gradient clipping at 1.0, and early stopping with patience 3 on validation loss. The best checkpoint (epoch 15, val PPL 199.64) is used for test evaluation.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Language modeling perplexity on WikiText-2 test set.}
\label{tab:q5_results}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Val Perplexity} & \textbf{Test Perplexity} \\
\midrule
N-gram (trigram, Laplace) & -- & 32{,}372.30 \\
LSTM (2-layer)            & 199.64 & \textbf{194.33} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The LSTM achieves a test perplexity of 194.33, dramatically outperforming the trigram model (32{,}372.30). The LSTM's perplexity steadily decreased across all 15 epochs, with no early stopping triggered, suggesting the model could benefit from further training.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Three seed phrases reveal the generation quality of each model:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Seed ``the president''}: N-gram produces a factually grounded but incoherent continuation (``of dartmouth college, rutgers university — titled `liu kang's dragon fatality'\,''), jumping between unrelated topics. LSTM produces syntactically plausible but semantically empty text (``of alphonse josé secretary of the first concerto''), reflecting its inability to maintain long-range topic coherence.

    \item \textbf{Seed ``in the''}: N-gram generates a locally coherent phrase (``united states on december 11, 1949'') by copying frequent trigrams, but the continuation drifts. LSTM produces fragmented output with rare tokens (``@-@ paced @-@ medical''), which are WikiText-specific tokenization artifacts.

    \item \textbf{Seed ``he was''}: N-gram stays on topic briefly (``pressed to the different conformation'') before drifting. LSTM generates syntactically richer but semantically inconsistent text (``when he left amateur wadham at offices'').
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Error Analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{N-gram data sparsity}: Laplace smoothing spreads probability mass uniformly over unseen trigrams, which severely inflates perplexity when most test trigrams are unseen. Kneser-Ney smoothing would reduce this substantially.
    \item \textbf{N-gram topic drift}: Since the model only conditions on two previous words, it cannot maintain discourse coherence beyond a single phrase.
    \item \textbf{LSTM repetition and incoherence}: With only 15 epochs of training on 2M tokens, the LSTM learns local syntax but not global topic structure. Perplexity of ~194 is reasonable but far above the state-of-the-art (~65 with transformer-based models).
    \item \textbf{WikiText tokenization artifacts}: The dataset contains markup-style tokens (``@-@'') from Wikipedia formatting. These appear frequently in LSTM-generated text since they are common in the training distribution.
    \item \textbf{No \texttt{<unk>} handling in N-gram}: Test tokens unseen during training all trigger the Laplace fallback, contributing heavily to the extreme perplexity of 32{,}372.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discussion}

The results confirm the fundamental limitation of count-based language models: data sparsity makes them brittle on held-out data, even with smoothing. The LSTM's perplexity of 194 represents a 166$\times$ improvement over the trigram baseline, demonstrating that distributed representations and recurrent state allow the model to generalize far better to unseen token sequences. The LSTM's val/test perplexity gap (199.64 vs.\ 194.33) is small and negative, indicating no overfitting. Further improvements would come from larger hidden dimensions, more epochs, or replacing the LSTM with a Transformer language model.

\section{Conclusion}

This report presented experimental results across five NLP tasks, comparing classical, neural, and transformer-based approaches under consistent reproducible conditions.

\textbf{Q1 (Text Classification)}: DistilBERT achieved the highest accuracy (91.15\%), confirming that contextual embeddings outperform both sparse TF-IDF and dense GloVe representations for sentiment analysis. Notably, TF-IDF with bigrams outperformed BiLSTM, demonstrating that simple baselines remain competitive when documents are long and labels depend on global n-gram features.

\textbf{Q2 (NER)}: BERT-base-cased achieved 91.18\% F1 on CoNLL-2003, outperforming BiLSTM-CRF by 9.6 points. Contextual embeddings proved critical for entity disambiguation (e.g., ``Washington'' as person vs.\ location), while the CRF layer in BiLSTM-CRF provided structural constraints but could not overcome the limitations of context-independent GloVe embeddings.

\textbf{Q3 (Summarization)}: BART outperformed TextRank on all six metrics (ROUGE-1: 0.44 vs.\ 0.36, BERTScore: 0.88 vs.\ 0.86). Abstractive generation produces more fluent and informative summaries, at the cost of occasional hallucination. TextRank guarantees factual faithfulness but fails when key information is spread across multiple paragraphs.

\textbf{Q4 (Machine Translation)}: MarianMT achieved a BLEU of 0.36 on Multi30k EN$\rightarrow$DE, while the Seq2Seq model trained from scratch scored only 0.015. The 166$\times$ gap in BLEU confirms that pre-training on large parallel corpora is essential for high-quality neural machine translation. The Seq2Seq model demonstrated expected failure modes: repetition loops and OOV sensitivity.

\textbf{Q5 (Language Modeling)}: The LSTM language model achieved a test perplexity of 194.33, a 166$\times$ improvement over the trigram baseline (32{,}372.30). Count-based models suffer severely from data sparsity on held-out text; neural models generalize far better through distributed representations and recurrent state.

Across all five tasks, a consistent pattern emerges: pre-trained transformer models dominate when sufficient compute is available, but classical and simple neural baselines remain valuable for interpretability and low-resource settings. The comparison between baselines and transformers is itself informative — it quantifies the value of scale, pre-training, and contextual representation, which is the central theme of modern NLP.
